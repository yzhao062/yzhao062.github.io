🔬 Research

Auditable AI Systems. When no ground truth settles the answer, you can still ask four questions of the record a system leaves behind.

Data · does the input resemble anything the system was built for?
Output · does it hold up against evidence?
Action · was it allowed?
Effort · was the work necessary?

Three of them ask whether something went wrong. The fourth asks whether it was needed. That fourth question is the one industry brings us: the companies that reach out want agents that cost less and finish faster. Building the record that answers all four is the lab's contribution to AI Assurance. The question underneath is what evidence is enough to rely on a system when ground truth is absent.

  1. Detection in Data: Anomaly, Outlier, and Out-of-Distribution Detection

    Here the question is whether an input departed from the conditions represented during development and evaluation. That boundary is hard to estimate when labels or representative examples are sparse. This is the lab's methodological and open-source base. It includes the PyOD ecosystem and ADBench, plus benchmark infrastructure for NLP. It also covers automatic OOD detector selection, modality-specific OOD methods for graphs, multimodal data, and video, and few-shot cross-domain OOD detection. PyOD is the flagship open-source artifact and is now in its third major release. When an incident involves distribution shift, these methods supply the evidence for whether unfamiliar input conditions contributed to it. Evidence at the action point establishes an action-only incident instead. Current research emphasis sits at verification of output and auditing of action.

    Keywords: Anomaly Detection, Outlier Detection, Out-of-Distribution Detection, PyOD Ecosystem, Benchmark Design, Multimodal OOD, Graph OOD, Few-Shot OOD, OOD Detector Selection

  2. Verification of Output: LLM Evaluation, Hallucination, Jailbreaks, and Guardrails

    Between the data going in and the action taken, foundation models add their own failure surface: behavior under attack, hallucination of fact and reasoning, and adversarial robustness of retrieval. Failures on any of these surfaces can propagate downstream when the model's output is passed to an agent or a user-facing system. My lab's targeted work measures these surfaces through jailbreak detection for vision-language models, causal analysis of hallucination, query-agnostic attacks on retrieval-augmented generation, and LLM-as-anomaly-detector benchmarks. Community efforts like TrustLLM are tracked as collaborative consortium contributions, separate from lab-led work.

    Keywords: Foundation-Model Trust, Trustworthiness Benchmarks, LLM Hallucination, Jailbreak Detection, RAG Security, Adversarial Retrieval, Vision-Language Models, LLM-as-Judge

  3. Auditing of Action: AI Agent Security, Audit Trails, and MCP Security

    Here deployed systems act through tools, APIs, and shared state, so what a system does becomes the primary failure surface. I work on auditability frameworks for reconstructing, checking, and attributing agent behavior after deployment, and on runtime control surfaces that intercept tool calls before they fire. Further work covers agent-specific failure modes and category-adaptive training-time defenses. Those failure modes include over-privilege, cross-user contamination, and the autonomy tax of defense training. These span the full lifecycle: pre-deployment audit, runtime tool-call mediation, and post-run attribution and recovery. My Auditable Agents position paper organizes these into five dimensions of auditability and three mechanism classes (detect, enforce, recover). These methods are moving from research into deployable open-source infrastructure that audits agents from before deployment to after a run.

    Keywords: Agent Auditability, Runtime Audit, Pre-Execution Mediation, Agent Failure Modes, Over-Privilege, Cross-User Contamination, Defense Training, Behavior Verification, Multi-Agent Systems, AI Agent Security

  4. Optimization of Effort: Agent Efficiency, Routing, and the Cost of Defense

    The first three ask whether a system went wrong. This one asks whether the work was necessary. For an agent run, failure diagnosis and efficiency analysis read the same record. A trace logs what each step did; GRADE adds the dependencies and reused results it omits. The trade also runs in reverse, and The Autonomy Tax measures it: defense training against prompt injection takes away an agent's ability to finish multi-step tasks. BaRP treats model choice as an online decision under shifting prices and accuracy needs, and DyFlow constructs and adjusts agent workflows as a run proceeds. The same question runs back through the detection line, where SUOD and the GPU-accelerated TOD cut the compute cost of running many detectors at scale.

    Keywords: Agent Efficiency, LLM Routing, Cost-Quality Trade-offs, Workflow Generation, Cost of Defense Training, Scalable Detection, Inference Cost, Dependency Analysis

👤 About

Biography

Dr. Yue Zhao is an Assistant Professor of Computer Science at the University of Southern California, where he leads the FORTIS Lab. He serves as Associate Co-Director of the USC Institute on Ethics and Trust in Computing for 2026-2027. His lab builds Auditable AI Systems across four audit questions: detecting anomalies in data, verifying outputs, auditing actions, and optimizing effort when no ground truth is available to check against. For each question, the work records what happened so that it can be reconstructed and checked after the fact. Auditability serves accountability and efficiency alike: the same evidence shows what a system did wrong and what it never needed to do. This is the lab's contribution to the broader field of AI Assurance. The program spans anomaly and outlier detection at scale, LLM evaluation where no reference answer exists, and the security and auditability of AI agents that act on their own. Its current frontier is agent auditability: open-source infrastructure that audits AI agents from before deployment to after a run. He also advises early-stage AI startups and is founding Auditable AI, a startup building the auditable layer for AI agents on an open-source stack developed by his lab.

Dr. Zhao has authored over 80 peer-reviewed papers. His open-source projects, including PyOD, ADBench, TrustLLM, agent-audit, and Aegis, together exceed 60 million downloads and 29,000 GitHub stars. As of August 2026, 5,560 public repositories and 159 packages depend on PyOD. PyOD is named by OpenAI, used by Apache Beam, Amazon, Walmart, Databricks, and the European Space Agency, and cited in 49 patents and five books. Official guidance names it across Europe, Asia, the Middle East, and Latin America, from defense ministries and central banks to national AI authorities. Brazilian federal and congressional procurement audits run it. His awards include the NVIDIA Academic Grant Program Award, multiple Amazon Research Awards, the Capital One Research Award, and the Foresight Institute AI for Safety & Science Nodes Grant. NSF grants and a Meta sponsored research agreement fund the lab alongside them. Those NSF grants include both phases of Pathways to Enable Open-Source Ecosystems (POSE). AAAI selected him for New Faculty Highlights, and his papers have won a Best Short Paper Award at ACM SIGSPATIAL and a Second Prize CCC Award at the IEEE ICDM BlueSky Track. [Aug 2026] New preprint and benchmark: CatchBench: When Can an Agent Failure Be Caught? An audit is usually limited by the record rather than by the method. CatchBench therefore puts one auditor's question to three information states: the configuration declared before a run, a growing prefix of its trace, and the finished trace. It scores 72 entrants over 1187 declared configurations and 1162 recorded runs. Most of the arena does not order, and the 71 contrasts that fail to separate are published unresolved rather than ranked. [arXiv] [GitHub]
